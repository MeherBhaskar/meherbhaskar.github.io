Your eval suite passed this morning. Every golden task green, same as last week, same as the month before. That is the problem. A golden set that never changes is not a sign that your agent is stable. It is a sign that your evals stopped measuring the world your agent actually lives in.

Here is the thesis: a golden set is a snapshot, and snapshots rot. The day you write an eval task, it captures one version of reality: the current product catalog, the current policy, the current phrasing users actually use, the current model behavior. Every one of those things moves. The eval does not. So the gap between what your eval measures and what your users experience grows a little every week, silently, while the dashboard stays green. A passing eval suite that measures the wrong world is worse than a failing one. A failing suite at least tells you something is wrong.

## The four ways golden sets rot

Rot is not one thing. It shows up in four distinct ways, and each one needs a different fix, so it helps to name them separately.

First, the world changes underneath the task. This is the most common form and the hardest to notice. Your eval task says "recommend a substitute for product X when it is out of stock." Product X was discontinued three months ago. The correct answer in the golden set is a specific substitute that no longer exists either. The agent now recommends something else, something arguably better, and the eval marks it wrong. Or worse, the agent has memorized the golden answer and keeps recommending the discontinued substitute, and the eval marks it right, while real users get a recommendation for a product they cannot buy. Either way the eval is no longer connected to reality. The task is not hard, it is stale.

This is not a hypothetical failure mode. Any agent that touches a live catalog, a live policy, a live price list, or a live set of user-facing rules is exposed to it. Retail assortments change seasonally. Policies get rewritten. Pricing changes. The eval task that was exactly right in March is exactly wrong in November, and nothing in the eval harness will tell you that, because the harness only knows what the task says. The task is the ground truth, and the ground truth expired.

Second, the model changes underneath the task. I wrote about silent capability drift in October, and eval rot is where that drift hides. Your golden set was written against one model's behavior. A new model version answers the same question with a different structure, a different level of detail, a different refusal style. The answer is still correct, arguably better, but it no longer matches the expected output shape, and the judge or the string match marks it wrong. Or the reverse: the new model has learned to produce exactly the kind of answer the judge rewards, and the score goes up while the real quality goes down. The eval did not get easier or harder. It got disconnected from what it was supposed to measure, because the thing it measures, model behavior, moved.

Third, the task itself was never as good as you thought, and time exposes it. A golden task written in a hurry encodes the author's assumptions: the phrasing they happened to use, the edge case they happened to think of, the answer they happened to consider obviously right. Over months of running, you discover that three of your fifty tasks are ambiguous, that two have answers that are wrong in a way nobody noticed, and that one rewards a shortcut that has nothing to do with the skill being tested. When the set was new, this was tolerable, because the set was small and you knew every task. When the set is a year old and nobody has read it since it was written, the bad tasks are load-bearing. They shape prompt changes, model choices, and ship decisions, and nobody remembers that task 17 was written at 11pm on a Friday and never reviewed.

Fourth, coverage rots even when individual tasks stay correct. Your users started asking a new kind of question six weeks ago. A new product line launched. A new failure mode appeared in production traces, the kind that shows up in the 2am pages. None of it is in the golden set, because the golden set was written before any of it existed. Every task in the set still passes. The agent still fails on the thing users actually need. This is the quietest form of rot, because there is no wrong answer to catch. There is only an absence, and absences do not show up on dashboards.

## Why nobody notices

If golden sets rot this reliably, why do teams keep shipping against them for months? The answer is that the incentives point exactly the wrong way. A green eval suite is a permission slip. It tells the team the deploy is safe, the review it is done, the manager the quality bar is met. Questioning the golden set threatens that permission slip, so nobody questions it. The person who says "maybe our evals are stale" is proposing work with no visible payoff and a real chance of turning a green dashboard red. That is a hard sell in any team, and in a team under shipping pressure it is nearly impossible.

There is also a genuine expertise problem. Maintaining a golden set requires knowing two things at once: what the world looks like now, and what the eval was supposed to measure when it was written. The person who wrote the task has the second kind of knowledge and often leaves the team. The person who knows the current world does not know the eval exists. The maintenance falls into the gap between them, and the gap is exactly where rot lives.

And there is a measurement problem. Eval scores are a number, and numbers create an illusion of precision. A score of 87 percent feels like a fact. But 87 percent on a stale set and 87 percent on a fresh set are different statements about different worlds, and the number does not carry a label saying which world it measured. Teams compare scores across months as if the yardstick were fixed. It is not fixed. It is quietly shrinking.

## The maintenance schedule

So what does maintenance actually look like? Not a heroic rewrite once a year. A schedule, small and regular, the way you would maintain any other piece of production infrastructure. Here is the one I would start with.

Every week, sample production traces and diff them against the golden set. Take twenty real user interactions from the last seven days, the messy ones, the ones where the agent struggled or the user rephrased or the session went long. Ask a simple question for each: is there a golden task that covers this? If the answer is no for more than a couple, your coverage is rotting, and the fix is to write the missing tasks while the traces are still fresh. This is the single highest-value habit in eval maintenance, because it keeps the set anchored to the world as it is, not as it was. It also has a side benefit: it forces someone to read real traces every week, which is the fastest way to learn what your agent actually does.

Every month, revalidate a slice of the set. Take a quarter of the tasks, read them by hand, and check three things. Is the expected answer still correct in the current world? Is the task still unambiguous, or have you learned something that makes the wording debatable? Is the task still testing the skill it was written to test, or has the product changed so much that the skill itself is different now? A quarter of the set per month means every task gets a human read once a quarter, and the work is small enough that it actually happens. The moment you make it a quarterly event, it becomes a project, and projects get postponed.

Every quarter, run a disagreement review. Take the tasks where the judge and a human reviewer disagreed, or where two human reviewers disagreed, and read them as a group. Disagreement is information. It usually means the task is ambiguous, the rubric is underspecified, or the world changed and the task did not. A task that two careful people score differently is not a hard task. It is a broken task, and it is teaching your agent the wrong lesson every time it runs. Fix it or delete it. Deleting a task is a legitimate maintenance action. A golden set with forty good tasks is better than one with fifty tasks and ten lies.

Every model change, re-baseline. When the model version changes, or the prompt changes in a way that affects output structure, run the full set and diff the failures against the previous run, task by task. The failures that are new are not automatically regressions. Some of them are the eval catching up to a better model, and some are the model catching up to a stale eval. The diff is the work: for each new failure, decide which of those two things it is, and fix the right one. If you fix the model every time the eval disagrees, you are training your agent to please a stale test. If you fix the eval every time the model disagrees, you are moving the goalposts. The discipline is to ask, for each failure, which side is wrong, and to have the evidence to answer.

## The versioning rule

There is one structural change that makes all of this easier, and it is the same one I argued for with prompts and tools: version the golden set alongside the things it measures. A golden set, a prompt version, a model version, and a tool schema version form a bundle, and a passing eval only means something for that bundle. When the model changes and the eval stays the same, the score is not comparable to last month's score, and pretending it is comparable is how teams ship regressions with a green dashboard.

In practice this means stamping every eval run with the set version, the model version, and the prompt version, and keeping the history. It means that when you update a task, you bump the set version and you record why the task changed: the world moved, the model moved, or the task was wrong. That record is the audit trail for the quality story. Six months from now, when someone asks why the score dropped from 87 to 81, the answer should not be "we changed something." It should be "we retired twelve tasks that were measuring a discontinued catalog, and the new tasks cover the failure mode that cost us the 2am page in September." That is a quality conversation. The number alone is not.

## The contrarian part

Here is the stance I will defend that most teams will resist. A golden set should have an expiry date, and tasks past that date should be quarantined, not trusted. Not deleted, quarantined: still runnable, still visible, but excluded from the headline score until a human revalidates them. The expiry interval depends on how fast your world moves. For a retail agent, three months is generous. For a fast-moving product surface, one month is more honest. The point of the expiry is not that every task goes bad on schedule. Most of them will revalidate fine. The point is that revalidation has to be a deliberate act, not an assumption. An eval task that has never been re-read is a rumor, not a measurement.

The objection I hear to this is that it is too much work. It is, a little. Reading a quarter of the set every month is maybe two hours for a team. Writing the missing tasks from the weekly trace sample is another hour. Compare that to the cost of shipping against a stale set: a deploy that breaks the thing users actually do, caught by no one, because the eval measured a world that stopped existing in August. Two or three hours a month is cheap insurance against that, and it is the only insurance that actually works, because the alternative is trusting a number that has not been checked since the day it was created.

There is a second objection, subtler and worth taking seriously. If you keep changing the eval, how do you know the agent is getting better? Is the score going up because the agent improved, or because you made the test easier? That is a real risk, and the honest answer is that you need a stable anchor alongside the living set: a small core of tasks that change slowly and deliberately, tasks chosen specifically because they test durable skills that do not depend on the current catalog or the current policy. The core is your trend line. The living set is your coverage. Both matter, and confusing them is the mistake. A single number cannot do both jobs.

## What to do on Monday

If your golden set has not been touched since it was written, do not start with a rewrite. Start with the weekly sample. Pull twenty traces from the last seven days. Read them. Write down every interaction that has no covering task. That list is your coverage gap, and it is usually longer than anyone expects. Then pick the five most common gaps and write tasks for them this week, while the traces are still in your head. That is one afternoon of work, and it will change the score in a way that actually means something, because the new tasks measure the world your users live in now.

Then schedule the monthly slice. Pick a date, put it on the calendar, and treat it like an on-call rotation, not a nice-to-have. The teams that keep their evals honest are not the teams with the best taste in eval design. They are the teams that made maintenance a calendar event instead of a vague intention.

The sharp version: your evals are not a monument. They are a garden. A garden you never tend does not stay the same. It overgrows, and then it stops being a garden. Your golden set is no different. The green checkmarks are not the quality. The maintenance is.

Which task in your golden set would you bet your own money is still correct today, and when did someone last actually check?
